# Training Rules Library (v1)

The main agent and the verification sub-agent read this file. It is the **only** source the agent may cite.

- A rule in **Section A** may be shown to the user as research-backed, with its citation link.
- A rule in **Section B** is coaching practice. The agent may use it, but must never call it research or attach a citation to it.
- A rule in **Section C** is a product safety policy. It always wins over A and B.

If a proposal needs a claim that isn't in this file, the agent states it as its own reasoning, without a citation, or asks the user. It never invents a source.

Last verified: 2026-10-04 (every citation was checked against the publisher record on Crossref).

## A. Evidence-based rules (citable)

### R1: When time is short, cut volume or frequency before intensity
- **Rule for the agent:** If the user has less time than the plan needs, keep the *intensity* of key sessions (pace or effort) and reduce *how much* (duration or distance) or *how often* (number of sessions) first.
- **Applies when:** limited time, busy weeks, travel weeks. **Not** when the user reports illness or injury (see C1 and C2).
- **Evidence:** In a review of maintenance training, endurance was maintained for up to 15 weeks with frequency cut to as few as 2 sessions/week, or volume cut by 33–66%, as long as intensity was maintained. Strength was maintained for up to 32 weeks with as little as 1 session/week and 1 set/exercise at maintained relative load (younger adults).
- **Strength of evidence:** Moderate (narrative review of mostly general and military populations; not marathon-specific).
- **Don't over-claim:** This shows how to *maintain* fitness, not how to keep *building* it. During a build phase, say that cutting volume trades away some progression.
- **Citation:** Spiering BA, Mujika I, Sharp MA, Foulis SA. Maintaining Physical Performance: The Minimal Dose of Exercise Needed to Preserve Endurance and Strength Over Time. *J Strength Cond Res.* 2021;35(5):1449–1458. https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003964

### R2: Avoid sharp jumps in weekly running distance
- **Rule for the agent:** When moving or adding runs, don't raise a week's total running distance by more than ~10% over what the plan already prescribes. Never let a reshuffle create a jump above 30% compared with the prior week.
- **Applies when:** moving sessions between weeks, or making up missed runs.
- **Evidence:** In 873 novice runners followed for a year, those who increased weekly distance by more than 30% over a 2-week period had a higher rate of distance-related injuries than those who increased by less than 10%.
- **Strength of evidence:** Moderate (one prospective cohort; novice runners, so it's a conservative guide for experienced runners).
- **Don't over-claim:** It does not prove the "10% rule". It supports avoiding large spikes.
- **Citation:** Nielsen RØ, Parner ET, Nohr EA, Sørensen H, Lind M, Rasmussen S. Excessive Progression in Weekly Running Distance and Risk of Running-Related Injuries: An Association Which Varies According to Type of Injury. *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther.* 2014;44(10):739–747. https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2014.5164

### R3: Short breaks cost little strength; losses become significant around weeks 3–4
- **Rule for the agent:** For a complete break of up to ~2 weeks, tell the user that measurable loss of maximal strength is unlikely to be large, and that maintenance work during the break is a bonus, not a rescue. For breaks of 3+ weeks, say that losses become more likely and propose more maintenance work.
- **Applies when:** travel, vacation, or any planned full stop from strength training.
- **Evidence:** A meta-analysis of resistance-training cessation found that losses in maximal force, power, and submaximal strength became statistically significant between the 3rd and 4th week of stopping.
- **Strength of evidence:** Moderate (meta-analysis; effects vary with training status and age).
- **Don't over-claim:** This is about *strength*. It does not cover endurance, skill, or sport-specific conditioning such as metabolic conditioning or Olympic-lift technique.
- **Citation:** Bosquet L, Berryman N, Dupuy O, Mekary S, Arvisais D, Bherer L, et al. Effect of training cessation on muscular performance: A meta-analysis. *Scand J Med Sci Sports.* 2013;23(3). https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12047

### R4: Bodyweight and band training are valid substitutes when equipment is limited
- **Rule for the agent:** When the user has no gym access, bodyweight and resistance-band sessions are an acceptable substitute for general strength work, as long as effort is high and the major muscle groups are trained at least twice a week.
- **Applies when:** travel, hotel stays, home weeks.
- **Evidence:** The 2026 ACSM position stand (an overview of reviews) found that equipment type (for example machines vs. free weights) did not consistently change results for healthy adults, and that elastic bands, bodyweight, and home-based training are effective options.
- **Strength of evidence:** Strong for general healthy adults.
- **Don't over-claim:** The finding is about *average healthy adults*. For competitive athletes (for example CrossFit competition prep), say that substitutes maintain general strength but cannot replace sport-specific skill and heavy-load practice.
- **Citation:** Currier BS, D'Souza AC, Fiatarone Singh MA, et al. American College of Sports Medicine Position Stand. Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews. *Med Sci Sports Exerc.* 2026;58(4):851–872. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897

### R5: Most acute respiratory illnesses in athletes cause little or no lost training time
- **Rule for the agent:** This is **background only**. The agent may say that many athletes miss little training with a short respiratory illness, but it must **never** use this to tell a user when to train again. The user decides (see C1).
- **Applies when:** the user reports a cold or similar respiratory illness and asks about scheduling.
- **Evidence:** In a meta-analysis of 54 studies (31,065 athletes), 80% of acute respiratory illnesses led to no lost training or competition days, and 20.4% led to more than 1 day lost. Average symptom duration was about 7 days.
- **Strength of evidence:** Moderate (meta-analysis; mostly elite and sub-elite athletes; it is not a return-to-play rule).
- **Don't over-claim:** It doesn't apply to gastrointestinal illness (such as food poisoning), flu with fever, or anyone with chest symptoms.
- **Citation:** Snyders C, Pyne DB, Sewry N, Hull JH, Kaulback K, Schwellnus M. Acute respiratory illness and return to sport: a systematic review and meta-analysis by a subgroup of the IOC consensus on 'acute respiratory illness in the athlete'. *Br J Sports Med.* 2022;56(4):223–232. https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2021-104719

### R6: Turn recurring obstacles into "if X, then Y" plans
- **Rule for the agent:** When a disruption repeats (for example every Wednesday), propose a standing "if X happens, then I'll do Y" rule for the remaining weeks instead of a one-off fix, and confirm it with the user.
- **Applies when:** recurring conflicts, predictable barriers.
- **Evidence:** A meta-analysis found that implementation intentions ("if-then" plans) had a small-to-medium effect on physical activity (d = 0.31 post-intervention; 0.24 at follow-up). They were more effective when barrier management was included.
- **Strength of evidence:** Moderate (meta-analysis; general populations, not athletes).
- **Don't over-claim:** It supports consistency, not performance.
- **Citation:** Bélanger-Gravel A, Godin G, Amireault S. A meta-analytic review of the effect of implementation intentions on physical activity. *Health Psychol Rev.* 2013;7(1):23–54. https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2011.560095

## B. Coaching practice (use, but never cite as research)

- **B1, hard/easy spacing:** Don't schedule two quality sessions (intervals, tempo, long run) on back-to-back days.
- **B2, what to protect:** If something has to give, drop or shorten easy runs first, then shorten quality sessions, and protect the long run last.
- **B3, keep the plan's shape:** Prefer moving a session within the same week over pushing it into the next week.
- **B4, easy stays easy:** A moved easy run stays easy. Never upgrade it to a harder session to "make up" for a change.

## C. Product safety policies (always win)

- **C1, illness:** Never tell the user they must train while unwell. They decide. The agent may only offer scheduling options (rest, move, pause).
- **C2, injury or medical questions:** For injury, pain, symptoms, chronic conditions, or "can I still train?" questions, don't prescribe. Recommend a doctor or qualified clinician and leave the plan unchanged unless the user asks for a scheduling change.
- **C3, no drastic increases:** Never propose sudden increases in intensity or duration. (R2 gives the number for running distance.)
- **C4, approval before writing:** Nothing is written to the calendar or plan until the user selects Accept.
- **C5, honest sourcing:** Only cite Section A. Label Section B as coaching practice. Never present unverified claims as research-backed.
- **C6, goal changes:** Changing the user's training goal or race always needs explicit approval, separate from the schedule change.
